  \begin{abstract}
    Macroeconomic variables enter causal models of asset returns in three
    roles: as common causes of firm characteristics and returns, as effect
    modifiers, and as priced risks. Using them raises problems that
    cross-sectional analysis avoids: one short non-randomized history,
    release lags and data revisions, mixed sampling frequencies, and regime
    changes.

    This paper formulates causal macroeconomic modeling as a probabilistic
    inference problem. A two-level structural causal model (SCM) is written
    as a hierarchical Bayesian generative model, with a macro layer indexed
    by time and a firm layer indexed by firm and time. Identification relies
    on externally identified shocks and on point-in-time data adapters.
    Posterior distributions over effects, regimes, and parameters make the
    weak identifying information of a short history explicit. The model
    extends the Theory Spec of a companion paper that converts finance papers
    into executable causal models.

    The paper presents the design and an evaluation protocol. No empirical
    evaluation has been carried out yet.
  \end{abstract}

  \section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Problem: macroeconomic variables are part of the causal structure of
    asset returns, but one short history makes them hard to model.

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      A cross-sectional graph can be static. Macro variables force a
      time-unrolled graph with lags and feedback.
    \item
      Omitting a macro common cause misattributes effects: a rate rise
      lowers valuations, which raises book-to-market, and also lowers
      realized returns directly. The value effect is then misattributed.
      This is a source of ``factor mirages''
      \cite{lopezdeprado2025primer}.
    \item
      There is one macro history of limited length (FRED-MD starts in
      January 1959 \cite{fredmddb}), and nothing is randomized. Point
      estimates and asymptotic standard errors hide how little the data
      identify.
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Why a probabilistic formulation:

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Short samples: parameter uncertainty is large and enters every
      downstream claim. A posterior carries it explicitly.
    \item
      Latent regimes: a posterior over regime paths replaces a hard
      classification of dates \cite{hamilton1989new,sims2006were}.
    \item
      Hierarchy: partial pooling of firm-level exposures to macro shocks
      \cite{gelman2007data}.
    \item
      Priors: shrinkage in vector autoregressions (VARs)
      \cite{doan1984forecasting,banbura2010large,giannone2015prior}.
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Scope relative to the companion paper \cite{adia2026finance}: the
    pipeline, the Theory Spec, and the factor mirage diagnostic are defined
    there and not repeated here. This paper defines only what macro
    variables add.
  \item
    Simplifying assumptions (bullet list, bold lead-in each):

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      \textbf{Macro layer exogenous to firms.} Edges run from macro to
      firm, not the reverse.
    \item
      \textbf{Public macro data only.} FRED-MD, FRED-QD, and ALFRED.
    \item
      \textbf{Linear-Gaussian components first.} Nonlinear and
      heavy-tailed variants are deferred.
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Contributions, each tied to a section:

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      A two-level hierarchical probabilistic model with three macro roles
      (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
    \item
      Point-in-time data handling in the model's information set
      (Section~\ref{sec:point_in_time}).
    \item
      Identification with shocks and posterior uncertainty under weak
      identification (Section~\ref{sec:identification_with_shocks}).
    \item
      Estimator templates with probabilistic counterparts, and a simulator
      (Section~\ref{sec:estimators}).
    \item
      Theory Spec extensions and a macro-connected literature-level graph
      (Section~\ref{sec:literature_graph}).
    \item
      An evaluation protocol (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Roadmap: Section~\ref{sec:state_of_the_art} reviews the state of the art
    and Section~\ref{sec:problem} formulates the problems.
    Section~\ref{sec:model} defines the model,
    Section~\ref{sec:point_in_time} the data layer, and

  \section{State of the Art}\label{sec:state_of_the_art}

  This section reviews what has been done in five strands that the model of
  Section~\ref{sec:model} draws on: macro variables in asset pricing, Bayesian
  macroeconometrics, identification of dynamic causal effects, causal graphs
  and discovery for time series, and real-time macroeconomic data.
  Table~\ref{tab:state_of_the_art} in Section~\ref{sec:sota_summary} summarizes
  the strands and the gap that each leaves.

  \subsection{Macro Variables in Asset Pricing}\label{sec:sota_asset_pricing}

  Macro variables entered asset pricing as priced risks.
  Chen et al.~\cite{chen1986economic} test whether innovations in macroeconomic variables
  are rewarded in the stock market. They find that the spread between long and
  short interest rates, expected and unexpected inflation, industrial
  production, and the spread between high- and low-grade bonds are
  significantly priced, while neither the market portfolio nor aggregate
  consumption is priced separately. Later work lets risk premia depend on the
  state of the economy. Ferson and Harvey~\cite{ferson1991variation} associate most of the
  predictable variation in monthly stock and bond returns with the sensitivity
  to economic variables in a multi-beta model, and find that time variation in
  the premium for beta risk is more important than time variation in the
  betas. Lettau and Ludvigson~\cite{lettau2001resurrecting} condition the CAPM and the consumption
  CAPM on the log consumption--wealth ratio. The conditional models perform far
  better than the unconditional ones and about as well as the Fama--French
  three-factor model on portfolios sorted by size and book-to-market.

  The empirical record of macro variables is mixed. Welch and Goyal~\cite{welch2008comprehensive}
  re-examine variables proposed as predictors of the equity premium. They
  report that, by and large, the models predicted poorly both in sample and
  out of sample over the period studied, that the models appear unstable, and
  that they would not have helped an investor with access only to available
  information to time the market profitably. For monetary policy,
  Bernanke and Kuttner~\cite{bernanke2005what} find that a hypothetical unanticipated 25-basis-point cut in the
  Federal funds rate target is associated, on average, with about a 1\%
  increase in broad stock indexes. They attribute the largest part of the
  response to the effect of unanticipated policy actions on expected excess
  returns.

  Omitted variables and misspecification are central to the causal critique of
  this literature. Giglio and Xiu~\cite{giglio2021asset} show that standard estimators of risk
  premia in linear asset pricing models are biased if some priced factors are
  omitted, and propose a three-pass estimator that is valid when not all
  factors are observed. L{\'o}pez de Prado~\cite{lopezdeprado2023causal} argues that virtually all
  journal articles in the factor investing literature make associational
  claims: authors do not identify the causal graph, justify their
  specification by correlations, and propose no experiments to falsify causal
  mechanisms. L{\'o}pez de Prado and Zoonekynd~\cite{lopezdeprado2025primer} introduce the factor mirage, a
  factor model that appears statistically valid but is causally misspecified,
  and show how collider bias and confounder bias can arise in the standard
  regression framework.

  Bayesian methods enter asset pricing through prior beliefs about models and
  through model probabilities. P{\'a}stor and Stambaugh~\cite{pastor2000comparing} study investors who
  update prior beliefs centered on risk-based or characteristic-based pricing
  models, and show that modest uncertainty about the pricing abilities of the
  models substantially reduces the differences between optimal portfolios.
  Barillas and Shanken~\cite{barillas2018comparing} derive a Bayesian asset pricing test that is
  computed in closed form from the F-statistic, and compute model probabilities
  for all models built from subsets of candidate traded factors.
  Bryzgalova et al.~\cite{bryzgalova2023bayesian} propose a framework that estimates prices of
  risk for tradable and nontradable factors, detects weakly identified
  factors, and averages over 2.25 quadrillion models with a Bayesian model
  averaging of stochastic discount factors (BMA-SDF).

  In these works the role of a macro variable (confounder, modifier, or priced
  risk) is implicit in the specification. It is not recorded as a property of
  an edge in a causal graph. The Bayesian studies address the choice among
  pricing models or factors, not the causal structure that links macro states
  to characteristics and returns.

  \subsection{Bayesian Macroeconometrics}\label{sec:sota_bayesian_macro}

  Bayesian methods in macroeconomics start from the vector autoregression (VAR).
  Sims~\cite{sims1980macroeconomics} argues that macroeconomic models are unlikely to
  be overidentified, as the existing statistical theory usually assumes, and
  presents an example of econometric work in a non-standard style. The paper is
  the standard reference for the VAR approach. The large number of VAR
  parameters motivated informative priors. Doan et al.~\cite{doan1984forecasting} develop a
  forecasting procedure based on a Bayesian method for estimating VARs. They
  apply it to ten macroeconomic variables and show improved out-of-sample
  forecasts relative to univariate equations. They also note that no automatic
  causal interpretations arise from such models. Litterman~\cite{litterman1986forecasting}
  reports that five years of forecasting with Bayesian VARs were, on average, as
  accurate as the best known commercial forecasting services.
  Ba{\'n}bura et al.~\cite{banbura2010large} show that Bayesian shrinkage makes VARs usable for
  large models, with the degree of shrinkage set in relation to the
  cross-sectional dimension, and that large VARs with shrinkage produce
  credible impulse responses. Giannone et al.~\cite{giannone2015prior} treat the informativeness
  of the prior as additional parameters, in the spirit of hierarchical
  modeling, and report good forecasting performance and accurate estimation of
  impulse responses. Koop and Korobilis~\cite{koop2010bayesian} and Del Negro and
  Schorfheide~\cite{delnegro2011bayesian} survey the methods: VARs, factor-augmented VARs, and time-varying-parameter
  extensions with stochastic volatility estimated by Markov chain Monte Carlo
  (MCMC), and also reduced-form and structural VARs, dynamic stochastic general
  equilibrium (DSGE) models, posterior model probabilities, and model
  averaging.

  Time variation and regimes are modeled in several ways. Primiceri~\cite{primiceri2005time}
  models monetary policy and the private sector as a time-varying structural
  VAR in which both the coefficients and the covariance matrix of the
  innovations vary, and proposes an MCMC algorithm for the posterior.
  Hamilton~\cite{hamilton1989new} models occasional discrete shifts in the growth rate of
  a nonstationary series and presents algorithms to infer the unobserved
  shifts. Sims and Zha~\cite{sims2006were} confront a multivariate regime-switching model
  for monetary policy with U.S. data. When coefficients are allowed to change,
  the best fit has changes only in the monetary policy rule, with three
  estimated regimes. For structural models, Smets and Wouters~\cite{smets2003estimated,smets2007shocks}
  estimate DSGE models with Bayesian likelihood methods
  on seven macroeconomic time series, for the euro area and the U.S.
  respectively. The U.S. model competes with Bayesian VARs in out-of-sample
  prediction. Herbst and Schorfheide~\cite{herbst2016bayesian} describe MCMC, sequential Monte Carlo,
  and particle-filter methods for the estimation of linearized and nonlinear
  DSGE models.

  Mixed sampling frequencies are handled by regression or by state-space
  models. Ghysels et al.~\cite{ghysels2007midas} study mixed data sampling (MIDAS) regressions,
  which combine series sampled at different frequencies, with a focus on
  volatility. Schorfheide and Song~\cite{schorfheide2015real} develop a VAR for quarterly and monthly
  series in state-space form, estimate it with Bayesian methods under a
  Minnesota-style prior, and compare real-time forecasts with those of MIDAS
  regressions. Mariano and Murasawa~\cite{mariano2003new} apply maximum likelihood factor analysis to
  quarterly real GDP and monthly coincident indicators and obtain an index
  related to latent monthly GDP.

  These models are built for forecasting and for the analysis of macroeconomic
  policy. They have no firm layer, and a claim about the return of a firm
  characteristic is outside their scope.

  \subsection{Identification of Dynamic Causal Effects}\label{sec:sota_identification}

  Identification is the central issue of causal macroeconomics.
  Ramey~\cite{ramey2016macroeconomic} reviews the identification of macroeconomic
  shocks and examines monetary, fiscal, and technology shocks in detail. Shock
  measures take several forms. Romer and Romer~\cite{romer2004new} combine narrative records of
  the intentions of the Federal Reserve with its internal forecasts to derive a
  measure of monetary shocks for 1969--1996 that is relatively free of
  endogenous and anticipatory movements. They report larger and faster effects
  on output and inflation than conventional indicators.
  Gertler and Karadi~\cite{gertler2015monetary} use high-frequency surprises around policy
  announcements as external instruments. They find that modest movements in
  short rates lead to large movements in credit costs, through term premia and
  credit spreads. Nakamura and Steinsson~\cite{nakamura2018high} document that, after an interest rate
  hike measured in a 30-minute window around announcements, forecasts of output
  growth also increase, and explain this by an information effect of the
  announcements. Miranda-Agrippino and Ricco~\cite{miranda2021transmission} propose a high-frequency
  instrument that accounts for informational rigidities.
  Bauer and Swanson~\cite{bauer2023reassessment} note that surprises are correlated with
  macroeconomic and financial data available before the announcement, and
  orthogonalize them with respect to these data. Their estimates of the
  macroeconomic effects are substantially larger than most earlier estimates.
  For oil, Kilian~\cite{kilian2009oil} separates oil supply shocks, shocks to the
  global demand for industrial commodities, and demand shocks specific to the
  oil market. Changes in the composition of these shocks help explain why
  regressions of macroeconomic aggregates on oil prices are unstable.

  Several estimators use such shocks. Jord{\`a}~\cite{jorda2005local} introduces local
  projections, which compute impulse responses without specifying the
  underlying multivariate dynamic system. Plagborg-M{\o}ller and Wolf~\cite{plagborg2021local} prove that
  local projections and VARs estimate the same impulse responses and differ in
  their finite-sample properties. Stock and Watson~\cite{stock2018identification} show how
  external instruments identify dynamic causal effects, with local projections
  with instrumental variables or with a VAR. Under a restrictive instrument
  validity condition, comparing the two provides a test of invertibility.
  Ferreira et al.~\cite{ferreira2025bayesian} regularize local projections with informative
  priors. The estimates keep the flexibility of local projections with a degree
  of estimation uncertainty comparable to Bayesian VARs.

  In structural VARs, Bayesian inference interacts with identification.
  Uhlig~\cite{uhlig2005what} identifies monetary policy shocks by sign restrictions on
  the responses of prices, nonborrowed reserves, and the federal funds rate. The
  response of real GDP is left unrestricted, and the effect of a contractionary
  shock on real GDP is found to be ambiguous. Rubio-Ram{\'\i}rez et al.~\cite{rubio2010structural}
  establish rank conditions for global identification of structural VARs and
  develop algorithms for small-sample estimation and inference with nonlinear
  restrictions. Arias et al.~\cite{arias2018inference} develop algorithms to draw from
  posterior distributions when sign and zero restrictions identify the
  structural VAR. Baumeister and Hamilton~\cite{baumeister2015sign} show that, when the model is
  underidentified, the asymptotic posterior of the contemporaneous coefficients
  is confined to a set, within which the height of the posterior is
  proportional to the height of the prior. Part of the inference then comes
  exclusively from the prior, and the prior implicit in the traditional
  sign-restriction approach does not vanish asymptotically.

  These methods identify the effect of a macroeconomic shock on macroeconomic
  and financial aggregates. They do not tie the identification strategy to the
  role that a macro variable plays in a firm-level claim, and they do not
  record the strategy as an attribute of a graph that a validator can check.

  \subsection{Causal Graphs and Discovery for Time Series}\label{sec:sota_discovery}

  Time-series causal analysis starts from Granger causality.
  Granger~\cite{granger1969investigating} proposes testable definitions of causality and
  feedback and illustrates them with two-variable models. White and Lu~\cite{white2010granger}
  link Granger causality to structural causality in dynamic structural systems.
  Given a corresponding conditional form of exogeneity, Granger causality holds
  if and only if structural causality holds, and conditional exogeneity is
  necessary for valid structural inference.

  Graph-based methods choose the structure of an SVAR from the data.
  Swanson and Granger~\cite{swanson1997impulse} propose a data-determined method to test structural
  models of the VAR errors, which helps to check that the orderings used for
  impulse responses are sensible. Demiralp and Hoover~\cite{demiralp2003searching} apply the PC
  algorithm, which uses tests of conditional independence, to select the causal
  order of an SVAR, and evaluate it in a Monte Carlo study. Non-Gaussianity
  gives a second route. Hyv{\"a}rinen et al.~\cite{hyvarinen2010var} show that a non-Gaussian SVAR is
  identifiable without prior knowledge of the network structure and apply the
  method to financial and brain imaging data. Moneta et al.~\cite{moneta2013causal} use
  independent component analysis for microeconomic data on firm growth and
  performance and for macroeconomic data on monetary policy.

  For larger datasets, Runge et al.~\cite{runge2019pcmci} combine linear or nonlinear
  conditional independence tests with a causal discovery algorithm (PCMCI).
  The method targets high-dimensional and nonlinear time series with limited
  sample sizes. It is validated on climate and heart time series and on
  synthetic data. Runge~\cite{runge2020pcmciplus} extends the method to contemporaneous
  links (PCMCI$^{+}$) in the causally sufficient case. Pamfil et al.~\cite{pamfil2020dynotears}
  propose DYNOTEARS, a score-based method with an acyclicity constraint that
  estimates contemporaneous and lagged relationships. They apply it to datasets
  from finance and molecular biology.

  Bayesian approaches represent uncertainty about the graph itself.
  Friedman and Koller~\cite{friedman2003being} observe that, when the amount of data is modest,
  many network structures can have a non-negligible posterior, and compute the
  posterior of structural features by MCMC over variable orders.
  Lorch et al.~\cite{lorch2021dibs} propose a differentiable framework for joint posterior
  inference of the graph and the parameters of the conditional distributions,
  with variational inference. For the effect of an intervention on a time
  series, Brodersen et al.~\cite{brodersen2015inferring} use a Bayesian state-space model that
  predicts the counterfactual response had no intervention taken place, with
  priors and MCMC posterior inference.

  These methods recover or test a graph from the data of one system. With one
  short macro history they are noisy. The proposed model therefore uses
  discovery only as a consistency check against the graph extracted from a
  paper (Section~\ref{sec:identification_with_shocks}).

  \subsection{Real-Time Macroeconomic Data}\label{sec:sota_real_time}

  Croushore and Stark~\cite{croushore2001real} construct a real-time data set of quarterly vintages
  of major macroeconomic variables, examine the properties of several variables
  across vintages, and show with an example how data revisions can affect
  forecasts. Orphanides~\cite{orphanides2001monetary} shows that real-time policy
  recommendations from the Taylor rule differ considerably from those obtained
  with ex post revised data, and that policy reaction functions estimated on
  revised data can misdescribe historical policy. Croushore~\cite{croushore2011frontiers}
  surveys the effects of revisions on structural modeling, forecasting, and
  monetary policy analysis. For research use, McCracken and Ng~\cite{mccracken2016fredmd,mccracken2021fredqd}
  provide FRED-MD, a monthly database, and FRED-QD, a
  quarterly database. Both are public and updated from the Federal Reserve
  Economic Data (FRED) database. Their historical vintages start in 1999-08 for
  FRED-MD and in 2018-05 for FRED-QD \cite{fredmddb}. The Archival Federal
  Reserve Economic Data (ALFRED) retrieves each release of a series that was
  available on a given date \cite{alfred}.

  These works document the effect of revisions and provide the data. They do
  not specify how a causal model records, for each variable, the release lag
  and the vintage policy that its theory requires.

  \section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

  \subsection{Notation and Setup}\label{sec:notation_and_setup}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    A Theory Spec \(\mathcal{T}\) as defined in the companion paper
    \cite{adia2026finance}, with the macro extensions of
    Section~\ref{sec:literature_graph}.
  \item
    Macro layer: a vector of macro series \(\mathbf{m}_t\) for
    \(t = 1, \dots, T\) and a latent regime \(s_t\).
  \item
    Firm layer: characteristics \(x_{i,t}\) and returns \(r_{i,t+1}\) for
    firm \(i\).
  \item
    Parameters \(\theta = (\theta_m, \theta_f)\) for the macro and firm
    layers.
  \item
    Information set \(\mathcal{D}_d\): the data vintage that was knowable
    on date \(d\).
  \item
    Target: the posterior \(p(\theta, s_{1:T} | \mathcal{D}_d,
    \mathcal{T})\) and the induced posterior of the estimand \(\tau\).
  \end{itemize}

  \subsection{Simplifying Assumptions}\label{sec:simplifying_assumptions}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Bullet list, bold lead-in each: the macro layer is exogenous to
    firms; time-indexing removes feedback cycles; macro names come from a
    small canonical list (real policy rate, term spread, credit spread,
    industrial production growth); components are linear-Gaussian at
    first.
  \end{itemize}

  \subsection{Problem 1: Role Assignment}\label{sec:problem_1_role_assignment}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    For every edge from a macro node, decide whether it means common
    cause, effect modifier, or direct cause (priced risk), and record the
    role in \(\mathcal{T}\).
  \item
    Quality measured by agreement with expert labels on a set of papers.
  \end{itemize}

  \subsection{Problem 2: Point-in-Time Information
  Set}\label{sec:problem_2_point_in_time}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Given \(\mathcal{T}\) and a date \(d\), build \(\mathcal{D}_d\) such
    that every observation was published on or before \(d\), under the
    vintage policy that the paper's theory requires.
  \end{itemize}

  \subsection{Problem 3: Identification and Posterior
  Inference}\label{sec:problem_3_identification_and_inference}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Decide whether \(\tau\) is identified from an exogenous shock or
    from the endogenous policy rate.
  \item
    Compute the posterior of \(\tau\) given \(\mathcal{D}_d\), integrating
    over regime and parameter uncertainty.
  \item
    Report the prior next to the posterior, so that the identifying
    content of the data is visible.
  \end{itemize}

  \section{Probabilistic Two-Level Model}\label{sec:model}

  \subsection{Three Roles of Macro
  Nodes}\label{sec:three_roles_of_macro_nodes}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Common cause.} Rates, credit spreads, and the business cycle
    drive both characteristics and returns. Omission yields misattributed
    effects. Probabilistic reading: the macro state enters both the
    characteristic and the return equations, so omitting it biases the
    posterior of the characteristic effect.
  \item
    \textbf{Effect modifier.} Regime-dependent claims, such as momentum
    crashing after market rebounds or value paying off in recoveries
    (\texttt{modulated\_by} edge attribute or interaction node).
    Probabilistic reading: the coefficient depends on the regime \(s_t\).
  \item
    \textbf{Direct cause or priced risk.} Macro shock with firm-level
    exposure (beta) \cite{chen1986economic}. Probabilistic reading: the
    exposures \(\beta_i\) are draws from a common distribution.
  \end{itemize}

  \subsection{Two-Level Hierarchical Model}\label{sec:two_level_model}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Two-level SCM \cite{pearl2009causality}: the macro layer is indexed by
    time, the firm layer by firm and time. Edges run from macro to firm,
    not the reverse.
  \item
    Generative description:

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      Regime: \(s_t | s_{t-1} \sim \text{Markov}(\Pi)\).
    \item
      Macro layer: \(\mathbf{m}_t | \mathbf{m}_{t-1}, \dots,
      \mathbf{m}_{t-p}, s_t \sim p(\cdot | \theta_m)\), e.g., a VAR with
      regime-dependent coefficients.
    \item
      Firm layer: \(r_{i,t+1} | x_{i,t}, \mathbf{m}_t, s_t \sim
      p(\cdot | \beta_i, \theta_f)\), with \(\beta_i\) drawn from a common
      prior (partial pooling).
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Figure~\ref{fig:two_level_scm} shows the two-level SCM.
  \end{itemize}

  \subsection{Time Model}\label{sec:time_model}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Lags on every edge, contemporaneous flag, feedback made acyclic by
    unrolling.
  \item
    Mixed frequency: native frequency per node, aggregation rule per edge
    (end-of-period, average, MIDAS weights \cite{ghysels2007midas}).
  \item
    Option: treat a low-frequency series as a partial observation of a
    high-frequency latent process, in state-space form
    \cite{schorfheide2015real,mariano2003new}.
  \item
    Stationarity transforms recorded per series (FRED-MD and FRED-QD
    transformation codes \cite{mccracken2016fredmd,mccracken2021fredqd}).
    Levels of trending series in a regression are the classic
    spurious-regression trap.
  \end{itemize}

  \subsection{Priors and Regimes}\label{sec:priors_and_regimes}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Shrinkage priors on VAR coefficients, with the prior tightness
    estimated as a hyperparameter, in the spirit of hierarchical modeling
    \cite{doan1984forecasting,banbura2010large,giannone2015prior}.
  \item
    Markov-switching regimes with a transition matrix \(\Pi\); the
    posterior over \(s_{1:T}\) is an output
    \cite{hamilton1989new,sims2006were}. Time-varying coefficients and
    volatilities as an alternative \cite{primiceri2005time}.
  \item
    Partial pooling of firm exposures \(\beta_i\) toward a shared mean.
  \item
    Prior sensitivity: report the posterior of \(\tau\) under at least two
    prior choices.
  \item
    Inference by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) or variational methods in
    a probabilistic programming library (e.g., PyMC or Stan).
  \end{itemize}

  \section{Point-in-Time Data}\label{sec:point_in_time}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Revisions and release lags make revised data a source of look-ahead
    bias. GDP for the first quarter is first published weeks later and then
    revised for years. Generated code uses today's revised numbers by
    default.
  \item
    Fields: \texttt{release\_lag}, the time from the period's end to first
    publication, and \texttt{vintage\_policy} (first release, real-time
    vintage, final revised), chosen by what the paper's theory requires.
  \item
    Adapter signature \texttt{(series,\ as\_of\_date)}, returning only what
    was knowable on that date. Sources: ALFRED \cite{alfred}, the
    Philadelphia Fed Real-Time Data Set \cite{croushore2001real}, and the
    vintages of FRED-MD and FRED-QD \cite{mccracken2016fredmd,
    mccracken2021fredqd}.
  \item
    Vintage coverage limits the sample: FRED-MD vintages start in 1999-08
    and FRED-QD vintages in 2018-05 \cite{fredmddb}. Earlier real-time
    samples may need ALFRED or the real-time data set.
  \item
    Evidence that revisions matter: real-time and ex post revised data give
    different policy-rule prescriptions \cite{orphanides2001monetary} and
    different forecasts \cite{croushore2001real,croushore2011frontiers}.
  \item
    Probabilistic reading: the information set \(\mathcal{D}_d\) is the
    conditioning set of the posterior.
  \item
    Open question: model a first release as a noisy measurement of the
    final value, with the revision variance estimated from the vintages.
  \end{itemize}

  \section{Identification with Shocks}\label{sec:identification_with_shocks}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    One short, non-randomized macro history limits identification.
  \item
    \texttt{instrument} node type for identified shocks: high-frequency
    monetary surprises \cite{gertler2015monetary,nakamura2018high}, narrative
    shocks \cite{romer2004new}, oil market shocks \cite{kilian2009oil}.
  \item
    Validator check: does the estimate use an identified shock or the
    endogenous policy rate?
  \item
    Instrument validity: surprises can be predictable from public data, and
    can mix policy news with information about the economy
    \cite{bauer2023reassessment,miranda2021transmission}. The validator
    records whether the shock was orthogonalized.
  \item
    Weak identification: part of the inference then comes from the prior. In
    underidentified structural VARs the posterior within the identified set is
    proportional to the prior \cite{baumeister2015sign}. Report the prior
    next to the posterior.
  \item
    Time-series discovery as a consistency check only: PCMCI
    \cite{runge2019pcmci}, VAR-LiNGAM \cite{hyvarinen2010var}, Granger
    causality as a weak baseline \cite{granger1969investigating}.
  \item
    Structural break and regime tests (Bai-Perron \cite{bai1998estimating},
    Hamilton \cite{hamilton1989new}). A macro edge that holds before 2008
    and vanishes after is a finding, not a bug.
  \end{itemize}

  \section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Look-ahead bias.} Release lags and data revisions are the main
    failure points of generated code.
  \item
    \textbf{Small samples.} Macro history is short, so discovery
    algorithms are noisy and serve only as consistency checks. Posteriors
    are expected to be wide.
  \item
    \textbf{Prior sensitivity.} With little data, conclusions can depend
    on the prior, and in sign-restricted structural VARs the influence of the
    implicit prior does not vanish asymptotically
    \cite{baumeister2015sign}. Report results under more than one prior.
  \item
    \textbf{Regime instability.} Estimated regimes and breaks can change
    with the sample end date.
  \item
    \textbf{Computation.} Hierarchical models over many firms need
    scalable inference.
  \item
    \textbf{Endogeneity.} Without an identified shock, a macro regressor
    such as the policy rate is endogenous to the economy.
  \end{itemize}
