    All You Need Is Luck

    The laboratory was built below the river because Giovanni Perri believed water muffled coincidence. He liked that thought: above them the city gambled with weather, traffic lights, ambition; below, computers and cables hummed with obedience bordering on faith. The air was cool, static-charged, humming with the low frequency of server farms. Monitors cast blue light across the glass observation chamber, the chamber where he spent most of the days.

    The machine reconstructed the past with perfect clarity, annotating every outcome, exploring all the possible hidden branches: promotions that depended on an elevator delay, marriages determined by a dropped glove, careers ended by a missed bus.

    When missing variables were assumed, more were added and the branches in the past multiplied.

    Perri tested athletes, generals, thieves, and prodigies. A sprinter who broke a world record because the rival in the next lane had stomachache that morning . In every case the decisive factor arrived earlier than effort: the birth hour, a teacher's migraine, a coin tossed by a distracted cashier.

    The machine measured competence by simulating all possible events in every possible branch. The results were always surprising and unstable. Nothing seemed to survive a small perturbation.

    The city above continued rewarding success and merit. Ceremonies, speeches, praises to crown discipline and hard work. The machine calmly highlighted the rainstorm that had kept stronger candidates home, the pothole that twisted a rival's ankle, the misaddressed email that cancelled an important meeting.

    "Success is a story we tell after probability stabilizes," Perri annotated in his lab book, his handwriting tightening as the weeks wore on.

    For three nights Perri slept in the glass chamber. He watched his own career unfold across simulations: every paper he had written emerged not from brilliance but from being bored on a train that once stalled outside Naples. Remove the stall, results changed, and the world-renowned Perri became somebody who failed to achieve tenure or start the subterranean laboratory.

    Perri always believed his supervisor chose him over a stronger candidate out of genuine recognition. He ran the simulation. The machine said the stronger candidate was offered the position first and declined due to a family illness.

    On the fourth night, he simulated the night he met his wife in a bar. The machine showed three other women he spoke to that evening. In branch 7491, he married the woman in the blue dress—they divorced after six years. In branch 7829, he married the redhead—two children, a good life, though he drank heavily. The machine ranked all iterations. The one he married was statistically the happiest choice, but not by much. The margins were small. Probabilistic noise.

    He sat with that result for hours. The blue light from the monitors caught her in his thoughts—the specific way she smiled, which he had believed was chosen by him, which he had believed meant something. The machine said it was a cascade of accidents: his friend's cancelled dinner, a coat check attendant forgetting her umbrella, a bartender pouring slowly on a specific drink.

    He reached for the keyboard to run another branch. His hand stopped. He did not run it again.

    On the fifth night, he ran a control experiment: himself. He input his own current state—this moment, sleeping in the glass chamber—and asked: what is the next significant choice I make?

    The simulation flickered. Then stabilized: 14:47 tomorrow, Giovanni Perri resignes from this position.

    The precision disturbed him more than the content. Not "might resign" or "could resign." Not a probability distribution. A fixed point. As if the machine itself were the attractor drawing the future toward it.

    He tried variations. Different starting conditions, different time horizons. No matter what he adjusted, the resignation remained. Fixed. Inevitable. The only variable that changed was whether he learned this fact before or after 14:47.

    At 14:20, his deputy knocked. Sara. Smart. Ambitious. She'd been waiting for him to fail.

    "The board is asking for results," she said. "They want to know if the machine works."

    Perri looked at her. He could simulate her career: she was in this room because a hiring manager's wife suggested her name at dinner. She'd survived three layoffs because a VP's daughter went to school with her. She'd rise higher than he ever did, not because she was brilliant, but because she had the right accidents.

    "It works," he said.

    "And? What does it show?"

    "That success is luck. All of it. Merit is real, but it's never the decisive factor."

    Sara smiled. "Then we keep this quiet. Use it to predict markets, select hires. Imagine the advantage—"

    "That's not what I'm going to do."

    At 14:43, Perri stood at the machine's primary console. Four minutes until the simulation's predicted moment. He felt something rising in his chest—a conviction about destiny, about character, about the choices that matter.

    At 14:44, he understood.

    The conviction itself was in the simulation. The machine had shown him the future—himself having this conviction—and the conviction was real and it was determined and it was exactly what he needed to make the choice the machine predicted. The machine had not trapped him. The machine had freed him into the precise choice he was meant to make.

    He turned the machine off at 14:46.

    Not to save the world—the last simulation showed humanity continuing perfectly well without ever knowing the truth. People would prosper, fail, thrive, and suffer, all while believing in their own agency. They would be happy.

    But he had seen the simulation that showed them knowing the truth. In those branches, people sat in corridors waiting for luck that, once seen, refused to happen. Knowledge created a paralysis that luck itself could not break.

    Because a world governed by luck was intolerable. But a world that knew it was governed by luck could not function at all.

    The screen went dark. The humming stopped.

    Above him, the river continued its indifferent flow, muffling nothing, deciding everything.
