Bin Analogy ML

  • This notebook uses the bin of red and green marbles, the lecture’s own analogy, to connect drawing a sample from a population to learning from data, without a larger running project
  • The pedagogical arc:
    • The bin as an unknown population, with true fraction μ\muμ of red marbles
    • A single experiment: is the sample fraction ν\nuν close to μ\muμ?
    • Monte Carlo simulation of the distribution of ν\nuν

Cell 1.2: Single experiment: is ν\nuν close to μ\muμ?¶

Goal

  • Draw one sample of N marbles from the bin, compute the sample proportion ν\nuν, and see how close a single estimate lands to μ\muμ

Description

  • Inputs

    • seed: random seed for the sample drawn
    • mu: true proportion of red marbles in the population, 0-1
    • N: number of marbles sampled, 10-1000

  • Panels

    • Population vs sample: bar chart comparing μ\muμ against ν\nuν, colored by how close the two are
    • Interpretation: current parameters, the sampled ν\nuν, and the error ∣ν−μ∣|\nu - \mu|∣ν−μ∣

Guided usage

  • Change seed several times, leaving mu and N fixed
    • Observe ν\nuν land in a different place each time, and the bar color flip between green, yellow, and red: one experiment is not reliable on its own
  • Raise N from 10 toward 1000, leaving seed fixed
    • Observe ν\nuν settle closer to μ\muμ and the bar turn green more often
  • A single experiment only gives a point estimate, never a sense of how reliable that estimate is
    • What is really needed is P(∣ν−μ∣>ϵ)P(|\nu - \mu| > \epsilon)P(∣ν−μ∣>ϵ): the probability that an estimate this far off would happen at all
    • Repeating the experiment many times, next, is how that probability gets measured

Cell 1.3: Monte Carlo simulation: distribution of ν\nuν¶

Goal

  • Repeat the sampling experiment many times to see the full distribution of ν\nuν, and empirically estimate P(∣ν−μ∣>ϵ)P(|\nu - \mu| > \epsilon)P(∣ν−μ∣>ϵ)

Description

  • Inputs

    • seed: random seed for the experiments
    • mu: true proportion of red marbles in the population, 0-1
    • N: samples per experiment, 10-500
    • n_experiments: number of repeated experiments, 100-10000
    • eps: tolerance ϵ\epsilonϵ defining “far from μ\muμ”

  • Panels

    • Distribution of nu: histogram plus KDE of ν\nuν across experiments, true mu marked, and the ∣ν−μ∣>ϵ|\nu - \mu| > \epsilon∣ν−μ∣>ϵ tails shaded red
    • Key insights: current parameters, mean(ν)\text{mean}(\nu)mean(ν), std(ν)\text{std}(\nu)std(ν), and P(∣ν−μ∣>ϵ)P(|\nu - \mu| > \epsilon)P(∣ν−μ∣>ϵ)

Guided usage

  • Raise N from 10 toward 500, leaving n_experiments fixed
    • Observe the histogram narrow around mu and P(∣ν−μ∣>ϵ)P(|\nu - \mu| > \epsilon)P(∣ν−μ∣>ϵ) drop: this is the Law of Large Numbers, ν→Pμ\nu \xrightarrow{P} \muνP​μ as N→∞N \to \inftyN→∞
  • Raise n_experiments toward 10000
    • Observe the histogram fill in and its shape approach the smooth normal curve predicted by the Central Limit Theorem
  • Replace “marbles in a bin” with “data points”, μ\muμ with generalization error, and ν\nuν with training error on N samples
    • The same result then reads: with enough training samples, training error is close to generalization error, the connection this bin analogy sets up for the rest of the course

Implementation cell3_monte_carlo_simulation_interactive()

  • Runs n_experiments independent trials of N Bernoulli(mu) samples each in _plot_monte_carlo_simulation(), recording ν\nuν per trial
  • Overlays a KDE on the ν\nuν histogram, shades the region where ∣ν−μ∣>ϵ|\nu - \mu| > \epsilon∣ν−μ∣>ϵ, and reports the fraction of trials landing there
